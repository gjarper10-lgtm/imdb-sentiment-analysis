6:00–9:00 · Viva answers

1. What is your main finding?

DistilBERT achieved the highest test accuracy, 93.29%. BiLSTM achieved 91.52%. MLP achieved 89.63% with much less training compute. This conclusion applies to this experiment and split.

2. Why compare these four models?

They capture different information: word identity in MLP, local phrases in CNN, sequential context in BiLSTM, and pretrained contextual representations in DistilBERT.

3. What is TF-IDF?

It weights words by their frequency in a review and how uncommon they are across training reviews. Our bag-of-words representation discards word order.

4. What are GloVe embeddings?

Pretrained dense word vectors learned from word co-occurrence. We use 300 dimensions for CNN and BiLSTM and fine-tune them. Therefore those models are not entirely trained from scratch.

5. Why BiLSTM and attention?

BiLSTM reads the sequence forwards and backwards. Attention pooling learns weights over valid hidden states and forms a weighted summary. Padded positions are masked.

6. Why did DistilBERT do better?

A plausible explanation is pretrained language knowledge plus contextual self-attention. We measured the accuracy advantage, but did not run ablations proving which component caused it.

7. How did you select the model?

Two learning-rate candidates per architecture, up to three epochs. Validation F1 selected the trial and checkpoint. Test labels did not select hyperparameters. The chosen checkpoint was then tested.

8. Explain precision, recall and F1.

For the positive class: precision = TP/(TP+FP), recall = TP/(TP+FN), and F1 = 2PR/(P+R). Accuracy is all correct predictions divided by all examples. TP means an actually positive review predicted positive.

9. What prevents overfitting or leakage?

Dropout, validation checkpoint selection and limited epochs help control overfitting. TF-IDF and vocabulary are fit only on training data. However, the audit found 91 unique cleaned texts shared between training and test, so we cannot claim a fully leakage-free split.

10. Why 512 tokens?

It limits compute and matches DistilBERT's position limit. Longer reviews are truncated, which can remove sentiment near the end. CNN/BiLSTM use word tokens while DistilBERT uses subword tokens, so the effective text lengths differ.

11. What are the main limitations and next steps?

One seed, a small learning-rate search, a three-epoch cap, truncation, and duplicate texts across splits. Next: use a deduplicated/grouped split, multiple seeds, broader tuning and a long-review strategy.

12. Is the demo retraining or calling an AI service?

No. It loads saved PyTorch weights with their fitted preprocessors, applies the same cleaning and tokenisation, and runs local CPU inference. Internet is only needed for first-time setup or a new training run.

13. What loss and optimiser did you use?

Binary cross-entropy with logits and AdamW. A sigmoid converts the output logit to a positive-class score. The decision threshold is 0.5. DistilBERT accumulates two batches of eight for a nominal effective batch of sixteen.

14. Is 93.29% an official IMDB benchmark score?

No. The synopsis uses a custom pooled 70/15/15 split. The official dataset split differs, and duplicate texts limit direct comparison. The report states both limitations.
